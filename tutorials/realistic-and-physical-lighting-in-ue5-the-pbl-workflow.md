### Unreal’s limitations [10:39]
**Transcript:** One thing we didn't cover in our last example is the material of the light itself.  In Unreal, the brightness of the light source and the impact it has on the scene are two  different things.  This distinction between two concepts that are one in real life is pretty common in game  engines and important to digest.  Unreal is only an approximation of how light behaves in real life and this has implications.  To address this issue, Epic released Lumin, which makes any emissive object cast  bounces of light in its surroundings.  The problem is that by default, we have no control over it.  This means that any emissive object will cast additional light on top of what we already  set in our light actors.  As emissive values are expressed in condolabry meso-square, we will usually use the Luminance  meter to validate our data.  This can be the case for the skydome or any other emissive material like computer screens  all at this place.  Where it breaks down is with emissive bulbs.  As filaments are extremely bright and hot, using these values as emissive intensities can  lead to unpredictable results.  This issue also happens in natural settings by dissociating the sun and the sky.  When you sample Illuminance data with the light meter, you capture the light of the sun going  through the atmosphere and layers of clouds.  The light scatters, and the fraction of its original intensity reaches you.  So when you read a specific value on the light meter, this is not the intensity of the  sun itself, but the leftover of that light that got to you.  It also contains the whole sky contribution, and like I said before, is impacted by other  factors of variation like the pollution, the surroundings and more.  The only system in Unreal that emulates what happens in real life is the sky atmosphere.  When using this actor, you leave the calculations to the engine.  The sun's intensity is driven by the sun's rotation and the atmospheric parameters.  This means that if you use the sky atmosphere, you shouldn't change the direction of light  intensity.  According to Epic's documentation, you should set it to 120,000 lux.  This value comes from the solar constant, which you can see it as a power of the sun  before it enters the atmosphere.  Using the sky atmosphere, though, comes with its straw packs.  By linking it to the sun, you lose control over the color and the intensity of the sunlight,  as well as the brightness of the sky.  The sky is one of the most important and biggest light souls around, and its diversity  and intensity in color is crucial to create impactful lighting.  I'm a big fan of using HGRI in my work, and I don't really like the look of the sky  atmosphere of volumetric clouds.  Now, there are ways to combine both, but they are not perfect, and you will lose the impact  of the HGRI on your indirect lighting.  I also want to be in full control of the amount of light hitting my scene, and with the sky  atmosphere, you dedicate that part.  As my approach is more about creating the most pleasing visuals, I usually tend towards  a simpler HGRI workflow, which is what we are going to dive into.  Keep in mind that it might not be the most physically accurate workflow, but it works  for me.  With that out of the way, let's start with our first study.

### Conclusion [27:02]
**Transcript:** And real, like any other game engine, relies on our approximations to simulate real life.  It is then essential to get to know the limitations of the tools and models you're working with,  especially when dealing with physically accurate data. The process of lighting in a physical way is far from being precise and seeking for perfect  accuracy is a trap.
