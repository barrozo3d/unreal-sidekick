## Structured Notes

### Core Technique
A learning method for reverse-engineering complex UE Materials node-by-node, plus a production Master Material / Master Material Instance / Material Instance hierarchy for organizing a background-art project's shaders.

### Summary
PivotOn (an environment/background artist) shares two things: (1) how they personally learned UE Materials — by opening real reference projects (Paragon Portal Guardian, a medieval village environment) and dissecting one node at a time rather than trying to understand the whole graph or the math up front, and (2) how they structure Master Materials in an actual project — split by rendering differences (domain/blend mode/shading model: water, foliage, glass, decals) or by asset-type/workflow differences (baked assets, tiling assets, tile-blends, landscape), then layered with an intermediate "representative Material Instance" tier between each Master Material and the individual per-asset instances, so common settings can be updated in one place and propagate everywhere.

### Key Steps
**Part 1 — How to study Materials (node-by-node reverse engineering):**
1. Open a real, well-built reference project (recommends Epic's Paragon assets and the free medieval village environment — both ported to UE5) rather than starting from theory.
2. Pick one functional area of the graph first (e.g. the moss/foliage-on-rock blending logic seen in frames — a `TransformVector` node converting a normal map to a different space, a `DotProduct` node) and isolate just that cluster of nodes.
3. For each unfamiliar node, test it in isolation (plug in a normal map, compare before/after) rather than reading the math first — build intuition from visual behavior, then look up the formula later once the use-case is already familiar.
4. Note recurring nodes across different materials — `DotProduct` in particular shows up everywhere (AO/moss masks, wall-facing detection, lighting, Fresnel, reflections — anything comparing two directions) so it's worth understanding deeply once and reusing that understanding.
5. Once individual nodes are understood, apply the same technique/function directly into your own materials rather than just moving on.
6. Don't try to understand 100% of a complex graph immediately — build up coverage incrementally across multiple study sessions/projects.

**Part 2 — How master materials are organized in PivotOn's current project:**
7. Split rendering-different materials into separate Master Materials: e.g. `MM_PFI_Default` (baked/general assets — static parameters only, no runtime UV controls since baked assets rarely need them), `MM_PFI_Foliage` (two-sided foliage shading model + Simple Grass Wind, exposes a Subsurface Color input specific to two-sided foliage), and a 4-layer Landscape Master Material (supports landscape grass type, height-based blending/"mounding" between layers via a reusable material function that takes Mask/Normal/BaseColor/Roughness/Specular and outputs through a `Set Material Attributes` node).
8. Rule of thumb for when to split into a new Master Material: different **Material Domain**, **Blend Mode**, or **Shading Model** (e.g. Water = Single Layer Water, Foliage = Two-Sided Foliage, Glass = Thin Translucent, Decals = Deferred Decal domain), OR a fundamentally different workflow/feature set (baked vs. tiling vs. tile-blend vs. landscape).
9. Avoid duplicating an existing Master Material just to bolt on one extra feature — this was called out as a real past mistake (a project accumulated near-duplicate materials from reused purchased assets, and eventually needed a full material-structure redesign because it became unclear where new features belonged).
10. Between each Master Material and final per-asset use, create one or more **representative Material Instances** (e.g. `MMI_PFI_Default`, `MMI_PFI_Default_Emissive`, `MMI_PFI_Default_Item` for drop items) with the relevant static switches pre-configured for that use-case.
11. Individual asset Material Instances inherit from the representative instance (not directly from the Master Material) and only override textures/per-asset parameters — asset creators never need to understand or manually recombine the Master Material's full static-switch surface.
12. Benefits of the representative-instance layer: (a) simpler, safer asset creation (no wrong static-switch combos), (b) one place to update shared settings for a whole category of assets (e.g. bump a "shine" intensity on the representative instance and every child instance updates), (c) consistent project-wide standards a lead can hand to artists ("use this instance for normal assets, this one for glowing assets, this one for drops").
13. Caveat: don't over-create representative instances either — too many becomes its own management problem; only make one for parameter combinations actually reused often.
